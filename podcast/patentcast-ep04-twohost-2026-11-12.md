### PATENT OF THE WEEK — NVIDIA's video game that watches your face and decides you're having a bad time

**MARA:** Okay. Picture this. You've died at the same boss eleven times. You're gripping the controller like it owes you money. And the game goes… "hm. She seems upset. Let me make that a little easier."

**THEO:** I have been this person. Nobody noticed. The game *noticing* is a lot.

**MARA:** That's an NVIDIA patent application, published November 5th. The game uses sensors to guess how you *feel* — your facial expression, the sounds you make, your heart rate and breathing, even how you hit the keyboard and mouse, plus your history, like failing at the same spot over and over. Neural networks turn all that into a guess: frustrated, bored, scared.

**THEO:** And here's the clever bit. It first figures out what *kind* of game it is. Because the same feeling means different things. Fear in a horror game? That's the product. Fear in a puzzle game? That's a bug.

**MARA:** So by the filing's description: frustrated, it can lower the difficulty — maybe only after several failures, so it's not twitchy. Bored, it raises it. And if you're *too* scared, it might do something gentle, like brighten the lighting so the hidden threats are easier to see.

**THEO:** "Excessive fear" is a setting. Somewhere there's a slider labeled "too much dread."

**MARA:** It can also decide whether to tell you or not. With or without notifying the player.

**THEO:** *(pause)* I'll let that one sit.

**MARA:** Backstory, as best we can find it. This application was filed July 14th, 2026, and it's a continuation of an application from July 2023 — so the idea has been in the oven for about three years. It lists three inventors, has twenty claims, and as of the end of July was waiting for an examiner. NVIDIA also has a separate application, which The Daily Upside covered back in January 2024, for guessing emotion from the *sound of your voice.* Same theme, different input.

**THEO:** Now, the honest fine print. It's an idea on paper. No accuracy numbers, no test results. And games adjusting difficulty on the fly is *not* new — plenty of games do it just by watching whether you keep dying. The new part is the camera-and-heartbeat part.

**MARA:** So: ships or drawer?

**THEO:** My opinion — only that — split decision. The quiet version, where the game just watches how you play and eases off, probably ships, because it needs no extra hardware. The "reads your face and pulse" version I'd put in the slow lane, because most players don't have a heart-rate sensor on, and plenty won't want a camera pointed at their rage.

**MARA:** And I'd say the "might not tell you" part is exactly where regulators and players will have opinions.

**THEO:** She's setting something up again.

**MARA:** I am. The Swamp.

### TERM OF THE DAY — "provisional application"

**MARA:** Term of the day: **provisional application.** Or, if you've been reading our Patreon: "the one with the asterisk."

**THEO:** A provisional is a placeholder. You file a rough, cheap, informal version of your idea, and the patent office basically stamps it with a date and says, "noted."

**MARA:** And then the clock starts. You generally get twelve months to come back with the real, full application. If you do, the real one can claim the earlier date.

**THEO:** It's calling dibs. Putting your coat on the chair at a restaurant, except the restaurant is the government and the coat is a document.

**MARA:** Why does it matter? Because when you hear "company X filed a patent," sometimes that's a placeholder, not a real patent. Last week we mentioned a company with a provisional on authorizing purchases made by AI agents. Placeholder.

**THEO:** And some of the glasses applications we've covered claim a provisional from about a year before the real filing. That's the dibs date.

**MARA:** So: "provisional" means "someone called dibs." It doesn't mean "someone built it."

**THEO:** It means someone *intends to.* In writing. With a date.

### THE SWAMP — your feelings are data, and who's holding the data

**MARA:** Back to NVIDIA's game that reads your mood. Here's the swamp. The inputs aren't just button presses. They're your face. Your voice. Your heartbeat. Your breathing.

**THEO:** Which, in privacy law, is a very specific family of stuff. Face and voice data are often treated as *biometric* data — the kind some laws handle with extra care.

**MARA:** And one detail from the filing, as it was described: the processing can happen on a PC, a console, or on a *server.*

**THEO:** "Or a server." Two quiet words. That's the difference between your face staying in your living room and your face going on a trip.

**MARA:** I'll add a piece of general background, not from the patent: the European Union's AI rules restrict emotion-reading AI in places like workplaces and schools. That's about those settings, not video games specifically — so a game might fall under different rules entirely. I'm not saying it breaks a rule. I'm saying emotion-reading is exactly the sort of thing lawmakers have already circled.

**THEO:** And the glasses that open for pre-order today have a camera and microphones too. Different company, different product, same question. Who gets to know when it's on? Who gets to say no?

**MARA:** My opinion: the rules arrive after the first big complaint, not before. So until then, the question to ask any device — what does it sense, where does it go, and can I turn it off?

**THEO:** And there's one answer to that question that doesn't involve a server at all.
