### PATENT OF THE WEEK — Google's glasses that remember what you looked at

**MARA:** Okay. Last week it was earbuds that remember what people *said.* This week, Google would like to remember what you *saw.*

**THEO:** The sequel. Sequels are always bigger and slightly creepier.

**MARA:** It's a patent application for smart glasses. The idea: you're wearing them, you look at something for a few seconds, and the glasses quietly save that moment. Later you just ask, "where did I leave my keys?" — and it can show you the last time you really looked at them.

**THEO:** My keys have been on the counter every day of my life. But the *glasses* would know. That's the dream.

**MARA:** And the filing is surprisingly thoughtful. By its own description it only saves an image when your gaze lingers on something for a set stretch of time — so it's not a firehose. It throws out blurry and duplicate frames. And — this is the interesting part — it's described as being able to mask faces, passwords, and other people's audio.

**THEO:** So the patent itself says, out loud, "this thing could capture things it shouldn't." In writing. To the government.

**MARA:** *(small laugh)* Which I actually find kind of reassuring? They *thought* about it. Whether it's enough is a different question.

**THEO:** Here's the backstory, and it's a good one. The application was filed in May 2026 and claims a provisional filing from May 2025. That same month, May 2026, Google stood on stage at its developer conference and showed off glasses with this exact kind of party trick — finding lost things — with product plans for later this fall. That's from press coverage of the event. I couldn't confirm that the patent *is* the product's patent. Could be cousins. Could be the same family.

**MARA:** Good flag. So: ships or drawer?

**THEO:** My opinion — and it's only that — ships, in some form. Because the company is already publicly demoing the lost-your-keys trick, which is not what you do with a drawer idea. What I'd bet is *less* likely to ship is the full "remember everything I ever looked at" version. That one runs straight into the same wall as last week's earbuds: other people are in your photos.

**MARA:** And that's the thing to watch for yourself. A camera on someone's face, quietly saving things — you deserve to know when it's on.

**THEO:** She's setting something up.

**MARA:** I am. Hold that thought till The Swamp.

### THE LONG GAME — a patent that showed up five years after the product

**MARA:** Fun twist this week. Normally The Long Game is a thing that sat in a drawer for years and finally *arrives.* I went looking for one that hit shelves this week and, honestly, the pickings were thin. I'm not going to invent one.

**THEO:** The most honest sentence in podcasting.

**MARA:** So here's the reverse. On November 3rd, the patent office granted Oura a patent for **detecting illness from the temperature data collected by a smart ring.** Oura already *sells* that. Their sickness-spotting feature is called Symptom Radar — it started as a beta in April 2024 and went out to ring owners that December.

**THEO:** The product came first. The paperwork showed up last. Like getting your receipt after you've eaten the sandwich.

**MARA:** And it's got a real backstory. The idea grew out of Oura's work on COVID — they published research in 2020 reporting that the ring saw changes in the data in about three-quarters of people before they felt symptoms. That's their number, by the way, not an independent one.

**THEO:** The application number in the 17-series suggests it was filed several years ago. I couldn't pull the exact filing date, so I'm not going to guess one on air. But the point stands: the grant took years.

**MARA:** And the *same day,* two other ring patents landed — Samsung's ring hardware and one from Ultrahuman for improving how a ring's optical sensors read your finger.

**THEO:** The ring wars have entered the paperwork phase. Nobody's punching; everybody's filing.

**MARA:** Ships or drawer on Oura's?

**THEO:** It's already shipped. Opinion on what happens next: the patent is a fence, not a product. Watch whether it gets used to keep rivals out of the "ring that spots you getting sick" business.

### TERM OF THE DAY — "continuation"

**MARA:** Term of the day: **continuation.**

**THEO:** Lawyers call it a continuation. Normal people would call it "the sequel the original keeps making."

**MARA:** Here's what it actually is. You file a patent. Later, you file a *new* one that points back at the first — same big idea, but with fresh claims, or a different angle. It inherits the original's date.

**THEO:** Think of it as a time machine for paperwork. This week there's a Samsung patent that's the latest in a chain that began with a **Korean application in September 2015.** Eleven years. The TV that names the song playing in your show — that idea has been kept alive in the legal bloodstream since before some of my coworkers could drive.

**MARA:** Why do people do it?

**THEO:** Two reasons. One, to keep tweaking the claims as the market changes. Two — and this is me being cynical, so, opinion — because it keeps the family alive so you can sue someone whose product shows up later.

**MARA:** So if you ever see "continuation" on a patent, it means: this is a sequel, and sequels have history.

**THEO:** And lawyers.

### THE SWAMP — the glasses question, and who gets a say

**MARA:** Okay. Here's my set-up from earlier. Look at two patents from the same week. Google's glasses quietly save what you look at. And **Apple** has an application for smart glasses that tell *bystanders* when the camera is on — a color-changing layer, a glowing wire around the lens, tiny light holes, even a physical shutter over the camera.

**THEO:** One company building the camera, another company building the "sorry, you're on camera" light. It's like a pickpocket and a bell, filed the same week.

**MARA:** And that's the swamp. The tech is no longer the question. The question is whether the people *around* the wearer get a vote — and right now the answer is whatever the light says.

**THEO:** Plus there's Google's sponsored-answers patent. Combine them: a camera that remembers, a chatbot that's trained to slip in ads. I'm not saying those two ideas will ever meet. I'm saying they're sitting in the same building.

**MARA:** Yes, and — this is opinion — rules tend to show up *after* the first lawsuit, not before. So until then, it's on you to ask where the cameras and mics are.

**THEO:** Which brings us to the one place where you *do* get a vote.
