## The full list

**Google — earbuds that remember the room** 🟡
*What:* Earbuds that capture what people around you say, so an AI can answer questions about it later ("what restaurant did Dave mention?"). *Built before:* This is Google making the world searchable — first the web, then the streets, now the air near your head. *Our call:* The tech ships trivially; the wiretap laws don't. They likely file it and wait.

**NVIDIA — the anti-cheat that watches your mouse** 🟢
*What:* Tells a human player from an automated bot by the subtle, messy patterns in how a real hand moves a mouse. *Built before:* NVIDIA's whole game-tech empire. *Our call:* Ships. Cheating is a billion-dollar problem and bots move too perfectly to hide.

**Samsung — an AI guard inside the storage drive** 🟢
*What:* A small transformer model living *on the drive* that inspects memory commands and blocks them if they look like ransomware. *Built before:* Samsung owns the whole memory stack, so it can bake defense into the silicon. *Our call:* Ships. Security that doesn't depend on you clicking "update" is the dream.

**American Express — a leash for the AI, at the kernel** 🟢
*(US-12530174-B2, published Jan 20, 2026)* *What:* Runtime risk control that sits *underneath* an AI model, intercepting what it tries to do at the operating-system level before it does it. *Built before:* Amex's long history of fraud and risk tooling. *Our call:* Ships. Banks will pay anything to not be sued by their own chatbot.

**Sony — AI that edits your video for you** 🟡
*What:* Automatically cuts raw footage into a finished, tailored edit — no human editor. *Built before:* Sony's media + camera empire. *Our call:* Ships eventually, and takes a job with it. The model doesn't get bored at hour nine.

**Cognizant AI Lab — three in one season** 🟡
*What:* (1) decision-recommendation AI that *evolves* its strategy as conditions change; (2) a system that auto-tunes a neural network's activation functions instead of a human guessing; (3) distributed machine learning that shares learned knowledge between machines through standardized metadata. *Built before:* 65 U.S. patents and counting. *Our call:* Real and clever, but invisible — these ship *inside* other products and never get named. The unsung plumbing.

**Wandercraft — exoskeletons that finally walk** 🟢 *(The Long Game)*
*What:* Wearable powered exoskeletons (patents filed 2021–2025) now reaching rehab clinics to help people stand and walk. *Built before:* Powered exoskeletons have been patented since the 1960s — it took sixty years of lighter motors, better batteries, and real-time balancing AI to make the old idea actually work. *Our call:* Already shipping. The future was filed before the batteries existed.

**The humanoid frontier** 🟡 *(watch-this-space)*
*What:* Across the field, the hot patent ground is **actuators and dexterous hands** — the parts that let a robot actually grip and move like us. Sony leads the humanoid corpus with hundreds of records. *Our call:* The bodies are coming; the hands are the hard part, and that's exactly where everyone's filing.

**The floodgates** 📈
Generative-AI patent *publications* crossed **500,000** in 2026. OpenAI alone had ~19 applications publish in a single recent 30-day window. The patent office stopped being skeptical of AI — now it's a stampede.

## The Schematic, Decoded — Google's listening earbuds

Picture the earbud as three boxes wired in a line:

1. **The mic (always-on capture).** It's not waiting for "Hey Google." It's just on, listening to the room, holding the last little while of sound in a rolling buffer — like a dash-cam that only keeps the recent loop.
2. **The memory.** That rolling audio (and a text version of what was said) gets held — on the buds, your phone, or sent up — so there's something to search later.
3. **The model.** When you ask "what did Dave say about the restaurant," the AI reads back through that held memory and answers.

The whole trick — and the whole problem — is box 1 never turning off. That's what makes it useful, and that's what records people who never agreed to it. Everything good and everything scary about this patent lives in that one always-on microphone.
