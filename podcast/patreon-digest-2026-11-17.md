## The full list

**Meta — glasses that read silent speech** 🟡 *(Patent of the Week)*
*Who:* Meta Platforms Technologies. *What:* US 2026/0253591 A1; priority Feb 25, 2025; published Aug 27, 2026 (older than our window). A wearable senses the physical act of speaking — facial muscle signals, facial vibration, small movements — to work out what you said, including whispered, mouthed and sub-vocal speech. It can pick or blend sensors (muscle electrodes, contact microphones, motion sensors, regular microphones), can tell whether a sound came from the wearer, and can use an earbud as an extra sensor. Sensors might sit at the nose bridge, temples or behind the ear; ML models may run on the device, a paired device or remotely. The analysis we read stresses it is **not** a brain-reading patent. *Built before:* Meta's Ray-Ban Display ships with the Meta Neural Band, a muscle-reading wristband; earlier filings covered contact-plus-acoustic microphones and a nose-pad vibration sensor. *Our call (opinion):* Ships eventually, in pieces. The filing confirms no product plan, claims may narrow, and real-world noise (fit, hair, chewing, motion) is hard.

**Apple — "HomeView" smart home hub** 🟢 *(The Long Game)*
*What:* Per MacRumors, a $299 hub with a 7-inch square screen, camera, and voice recognition for up to six people; launch Friday Nov 13, 2026 in the US, UK, Canada, Australia, NZ, India, Singapore and South Africa — not the EU (Siri AI not offered there). *Built before:* We found **no patent for this device**; the only patent mention was a trade-press note that Apple filings reference professional smart-home installers. The wait is a *product* wait: reportedly first targeted around March 2025, then slipped (spring 2026, then September 2026) mostly over Siri's AI delays, per Bloomberg's Mark Gurman as relayed by trade press; units were reportedly being tested internally in 2024. Roughly 20 months past the first target. We saw the "HomeView" name on a single source. *Our call:* Already shipping.

*What:* Per MacRumors, filed Feb 5, 2026, published Aug 6, 2026 (older): a system that decides in real time whether to interrupt what you're doing on a device. *Our call:* Likely a software feature if it ships; details thin.

**Oura — one charging dock for every ring size** 🟢
*What:* Granted U.S. patent per the roundup; number not given. *Our call:* Ships quietly. Nobody tweets about docks.

**Samsung — ring that calibrates to your finger** 🟢
*What:* A short calibration gesture so the ring knows how it sits, keeping gesture and activity detection reliable (per the5krunner; number not given). *Our call:* Ships; rings rotate and this fixes it.

**Samsung — ring that identifies its wearer** 🟡
*What:* Checks a biometric reading against stored data, then sets the ring up for that person (per the5krunner; number not given). A separate Samsung ring-type wearable patent, US 12,783,008, was granted Nov 3, 2026 (title only). *Our call:* Plausible; thin.

**Samsung — sensors beside the watch display** 🟡
*What:* Places sensors under the same lens as the display, not just on the caseback — useful for touch-based readings like ECG (per the5krunner). *Our call:* A layout change; could ship.

*What:* US 12,773,119 (filed 2022): recognizes a genuine jump in wrist heart rate instead of averaging it away, for better interval data. A version may already be on current watches (per the roundup). *Our call:* Ships / possibly already shipping.

## The Schematic, Decoded — Meta's silent-speech glasses

Three short steps:

1. **Feel the speaking.** Even when you mouth words without sound, your jaw and face muscles still move and vibrate a little. Tiny sensors on the nose bridge or temples feel that — like a piano tuner resting a hand on the wood to feel a note you can't hear.
2. **Pick the right sense.** The glasses blend the clues — muscle signals, vibration, motion, and plain microphones — depending on whether you're talking, whispering, or only mouthing. It can also check "was that *me*?" and ignore everyone else.
3. **Turn it into words.** A machine-learning model maps the pattern to text or a command. It might run on the glasses, your phone, or a server — the filing allows all three.

Step 3's "where does it run?" is the privacy question. This isn't mind-reading, but it's a sensor learning how *you* speak.
